The O-Ring Run

Early in the story, a filtration emergency sends Jodie beyond her assigned section to retrieve a repair gasket. Adapted from the expanded story treatment.

The hiss was quiet enough that the others kept working.

Brooks did not.

He stood beneath the bank of gauges with one hand on the metal housing, listening through his fingertips. The filtration room carried its usual sounds around him: fans dragging air through tired ducts, boots on the grated floor, somebody arguing over a wrench. The Tower had made noise all his life. Most of it was harmless. You learned the difference if you wanted to grow old.

This noise had an edge to it.

He bent, opened the side port, and checked the needle again.

There it was. A hairline split in the seal. Small enough to insult him. Large enough to turn ugly in an hour or two.

"What is it?" Jodie asked.

He looked over. She had stopped halfway through carrying a stack of filter cloths, eyes already bright with the possibility that something interesting might be happening. She had that look more often than was healthy in Filtration.

"A leak," Brooks said.

The word brought people over. Workers gathered in the narrow aisle, careful not to look frightened until somebody else did first.

Brooks laid out the choices. They could bind it with the materials they had. They could layer a temporary patch over the split and pray the pressure held. Both would buy time. Neither would solve the problem.

Then he named the proper repair.

"O-ring gasket," he said. "Records and Storage. East side. Several dozen levels from here."

Nobody spoke.

The east side may as well have been another planet. Filtration was one of the Tower's highest service sections, sealed into the mechanical half of the complex. The middle levels had corridors, bridges, and old elevators linking the two working sides at certain points, but every route had rules attached to it. You went where you were assigned. You knew the people assigned there. You did not get curious.

Jodie set down the filter cloths.

"I'll go."

Brooks looked at her for a moment. He had known her long enough to see the answer arrive before the question did.

"You don't know the route."

"You do."

"I know pieces of it."

"That's more than I know."

Somebody made a doubtful sound. A girl from Filtration crossing the Tower on an unauthorized repair run was the kind of sentence that usually ended with everyone pretending they had never heard it.

Brooks kept his eyes on Jodie. He had collected directions for decades, the way other people collected stories. Names. Landmarks. Warnings. A sailor called Copperfield who knew where a wall came loose. A water maintainer called Houdini who could get a person through quarters without being seen. Tank Four to Tank Five. Twelve seconds after the second valve closed.

Details had value. Nobody had ever thought to give Brooks a reason to write them down.

"If you go," he said, "you do exactly what I tell you."

Jodie tried for solemnity and nearly managed it.

"Exactly."

"And you do not improvise."

"I hate improvising."

Brooks gave her a look.

"I dislike it very much," she amended.

The lowest duct was already open by the time she reached it. Someone had removed the panel and left the fasteners in a neat row on the floor. Below, the shaft dropped into darkness, a narrow black throat breathing colder air up at her.

"Godspeed," someone said behind her.

Jodie swung one leg inside.

"I don't need it."

Then she pushed off.

The duct pitched hard. She slid on her back, boots scraping, shoulder striking metal whenever the passage narrowed. It lasted only seconds. It felt longer. At the grate she hit hard enough to bite her tongue, wrestled the latch open, and looked down.

Ten feet.

"Let's go," she told herself.

She dropped.

Pain rose through both knees when she landed, but she had already decided that stopping would make it worse. She limped onto the catwalk of the sail deployment level.

Ropes crossed the space in every direction. Thick cables, tension lines, pulleys, guides, winches. They rose and disappeared into the ceiling, then plunged through the floors below. People elsewhere in the Tower called them the ropes of life. The workers who had to maintain them called them other things.

Jodie kept her hands close to her body and moved beneath them.

At the next level, a lean man waited by a removable wall panel. He looked at her once, then at the grime on her Filtration clothes.

"Copperfield?" she asked.

"Depends who's asking."

"Brooks. Filtration."

The name did the work. He pulled the panel free.

Behind it was a shaft barely wide enough for a person. Jodie stared into it.

"That is not a route," she said.

"It goes somewhere."

"Everything goes somewhere if you fall far enough."

Copperfield almost smiled. "You're supposed to be careful."

"Who told you that?"

"Brooks."

She climbed in anyway.

The shaft smelled of dust and old insulation. It squeezed her shoulders and stole the light after only a few feet. She lowered herself until her boots found the next panel, kicked it twice, and fell through into a dim sleeping quarter.

A heavyset man was waiting there with a laundry cart. He glanced at her, glanced at the hole in the wall, and nodded toward the cart.

"Houdini," Jodie said.

"Don't call me that where people can hear."

He covered her with folded linens and pushed the cart through the water maintainers' living level. Jodie lay beneath the cloth, watching strips of ceiling slide past. She could hear voices through the stack: familiar laughter, someone complaining about a blocked drain, a child reciting a lesson in the next room. It was not so different from Filtration. That was the unsettling part. The forbidden parts of the Tower sounded like people.

Houdini stopped at the broad central spine where the living and mechanical halves met. Beyond the corner, workers crossed in both directions beneath bright signs and watchful cameras.

"I'm out," he said.

Jodie sat up. "Out?"

"I don't have a way through that."

A man in water-maintenance gear passed by: hooded suit, filtered mask, gloves. Houdini caught his sleeve.

"Francois. Need the suit."

Francois looked at Jodie, then at Houdini. He did not ask why. He took off the mask and outer gear, handing them over piece by piece.

"You owe me," Houdini said.

"I know," Francois said.

Jodie pulled on the suit. It smelled like wet metal and somebody else's sweat. The mask pressed her breath back into her face.

She stepped into the central corridor with the water crews.

Nobody looked twice.

On the other side, alone again, she found the storage approach. Brooks's instructions came back in pieces, spoken so many times she could hear the rhythm of his voice between the tanks.

Tank Four to Tank Five. Wait for the purge cycle. Count twelve after the second valve.

The tanks stood close enough to make the service passage feel carved out of the Tower rather than built inside it. Jodie watched the gauges. One valve closed with a deep metallic knock. Then another.

She counted under her breath.

"One. Two. Three."

The pressure changed. Somewhere inside the walls, water rushed away.

"Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve."

She ran.

The opening ahead of her was narrower than it had looked from the first tank. She turned sideways, shoved through, and felt the system wake behind her. Metal groaned. A blast of air struck the back of her neck as the passage sealed.

She stood against the wall, laughing once in disbelief.

Then the air changed.

Filtration air was clean enough to forget. This was impossible to forget. Excrement. Mold. Wet vegetation. Something sweet beginning to rot and something rotten becoming sweet again. The smells layered until she could not sort them apart.

Agro.

Jodie pushed through the access hatch and missed the footing on the other side.

She dropped into a cavern of grow lights, hanging lines, and green climbing up every vertical surface. Her shoulder struck a support. Her hip clipped a tray. A bank of lights swung wildly above her. She landed in a mess of soil and roots with enough noise to wake the dead.

An Agro worker came running.

He was young. Softer-looking than the people Jodie knew from Filtration. His eyes went wide when he saw her, then narrowed as he took in the open hatch, the water suit half-twisted around her, and the approaching voices beyond the rows.

"Can you stand?" he asked.

Jodie nodded, though the room tilted.

He grabbed her elbow and pulled her beneath a curtain of hanging vines. Then he began gathering the broken pieces of the light rig as quickly as he could.

Two workers arrived at a run.

"What happened?" one demanded.

The young man looked at the wreckage, then at Jodie hidden in the green.

"I bumped it," he said.

"You did all this?"

He swallowed. "I was moving too fast."

The other worker swore and started calling for a supervisor. Neither looked closely enough at the vines to find her.

Jodie stared at him. He had taken the blame without even knowing her name.

When the voices moved away, he let out a breath.

"You lied," she said.

He looked genuinely puzzled. "Yeah."

"Why?"

He glanced toward the damaged lights. "You needed help."

For a second, Jodie had nothing to say.

The young man pointed across the Agro floor to a shallow industrial cradle beneath a feed chute.

"That goes to Distribution," he said. "You can get out that way."

Jodie looked at it. "What goes in there?"

He lifted one shoulder.

That did not help.

She climbed in anyway.

The valve overhead opened.

Something warm and thick poured across her boots, then her knees. It climbed slowly, sticking to her clothes, her arms, her neck. Jodie clenched her jaw and held still as it reached her chin.

She tasted it.

Honey.

"Oh, thank God," she whispered.

The cradle shuddered and began to descend.
